Eisenhower Matrix vs RICE vs MoSCoW: Which Prioritization Framework to Use

« Back to all articles

Most productivity advice fails at the same point: you have a long list, everything looks urgent, and you start with whatever is easiest. Prioritization frameworks solve that problem by giving you a consistent way to decide. Three of the most useful are the Eisenhower Matrix, RICE and MoSCoW. They are covered alongside deep work and habit techniques in the Career & Productivity Cheatsheet.

The Eisenhower Matrix: urgent versus important

Sort every task into one of four boxes:

  • Urgent and important: do it now.
  • Important, not urgent: schedule it. This is where growth happens.
  • Urgent, not important: delegate it or handle it quickly.
  • Neither: drop it or defer it indefinitely.

The matrix works best for daily decisions. Its weakness is that it does not compare two important tasks against each other, which is where RICE helps.

RICE: scoring projects with numbers

RICE stands for Reach, Impact, Confidence and Effort. The score is:

RICE score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort

Suppose a feature will reach 2,000 users per quarter, has an impact of 2 on a 0.25 to 3 scale, you are 80 percent confident, and it takes 4 person-weeks. The score is (2,000 × 2 × 0.8) ÷ 4 = 800. Score every candidate project the same way, and the ranking becomes a conversation about assumptions instead of opinions. RICE is useful for product and business teams, and for freelancers choosing between client projects.

MoSCoW: agreeing on scope

MoSCoW sorts requirements into four groups:

  • Must have: the project fails without it.
  • Should have: important, but a workaround exists.
  • Could have: nice to include if time allows.
  • Won't have (this time): explicitly out of scope.

MoSCoW is strongest when a group needs to agree on what a deadline will cover. The Won't-have list matters most, because it prevents scope from growing silently.

Protecting the time you prioritized

A ranked list means little if your calendar is full of shallow tasks. Two techniques help:

  1. Time blocking: assign a specific block to your most important task, and treat it like a meeting.
  2. Pomodoro or 50/10 cycles: work in focused intervals, such as 25 minutes on and 5 off, or 50 on and 10 off, to make deep work sustainable.

Cal Newport's work on deep work argues that focused effort on cognitively demanding tasks produces disproportionate results. Time blocking is one practical way to create that focus.

Building habits around the work

Habit stacking attaches a new habit to an existing one, using the formula "After [current habit], I will [new habit]." James Clear's 2-minute rule suggests starting any new habit with a version that takes two minutes or less, so it is hard to skip. These techniques are useful because prioritization decisions only matter if you repeat them.

Which one should you start with?

Use Eisenhower for daily triage, RICE when you have several substantial projects competing for time, and MoSCoW when you need to define a deliverable. Many people use all three in sequence: sort the week with Eisenhower, score projects with RICE, then define scope with MoSCoW.
