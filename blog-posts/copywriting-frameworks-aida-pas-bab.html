AIDA, PAS and BAB: Copywriting Frameworks With Real Sales Examples

« Back to all articles

Good copy is structure as much as style. Three frameworks cover most sales pages for digital products: AIDA, PAS and BAB. The Creator Economy & Digital Marketing Cheatsheet covers all three in its copywriting module, along with SEO hooks and conversion benchmarks. This guide shows each framework applied to the same product, so the differences are easy to see.

The example product is a printable weekly planner for freelancers.

AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

AIDA moves the reader through four stages in order. It works well for products with a clear visual or emotional appeal.

  • Attention: "Your week, on one page."
  • Interest: "Every client, deadline and invoice in a single view, without opening five apps."
  • Desire: "Imagine finishing Friday with nothing left floating in your head."
  • Action: "Download the Freelancer Weekly Planner today."

PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solution

PAS starts with a pain the reader already feels, deepens it, and then offers relief. It suits problem-driven products.

  • Problem: "Missed a deadline because it lived in an email you forgot?"
  • Agitate: "One missed invoice can delay your payment by a month, and the scramble costs evenings you should have spent resting."
  • Solution: "The Freelancer Weekly Planner puts every deadline on one page, so nothing depends on memory."

BAB: Before, After, Bridge

BAB contrasts the current situation with a better one, then shows how the product connects them. It works well for transformation promises.

  • Before: "Your week runs on sticky notes, scattered apps and guesswork."
  • After: "Your week is planned on paper before Monday starts, and you can see the whole thing at a glance."
  • Bridge: "The Freelancer Weekly Planner gives you a repeatable layout, so the planning takes twenty minutes instead of two hours."

Test before you commit

Write two versions of your sales page headline, one per framework, and run them against each other on the same traffic. Track email opt-in rate and purchase rate rather than guessing which reads better. A small test often produces a clearer answer than hours of debate.

Why a framework beats inspiration when you write sales copy

Most people who write sales pages start with a blank screen and hope a good sentence will arrive. Sometimes it does, but more often the result wanders, mixes several messages, and ends without a clear request. A copywriting framework gives you a sequence to follow, so every paragraph has a job. AIDA, PAS and BAB are three of the most durable sequences, and they work because they mirror the questions a buyer asks in their head: Is this for me? Do I have this problem? Will this actually help?

I think frameworks are most valuable for small digital shops, where one person writes every product page. They turn writing from an emotional gamble into a repeatable process. Once you know the sequence, you can draft a page in an hour, test a second version, and compare the two results honestly.

Write the headline first, then choose the framework

Before deciding between AIDA, PAS and BAB, write the headline and the first sentence. The headline carries most of the attention a visitor will give you, so it deserves the most effort. Ask yourself what the buyer is looking for in the moment they type a search, and reflect that language back to them. A headline such as "A weekly planner for freelancers who juggle invoices and deadlines" tells the reader immediately whether the page is for them.

Once the headline is set, the framework choice becomes easier. If the buyer is already aware of a painful problem, PAS usually fits. If the product is more about an appealing outcome, BAB often works better. AIDA is a good general structure when the product needs a strong visual or lifestyle hook, such as a printable journal or a decorative wall planner.

Common mistakes that make copy sound generic

The most frequent mistake is describing the product instead of the buyer's situation. A list of pages, sizes and file formats matters, but it should come after the reader understands why the product matters to them. Another common error is overpromising. Claims such as "guaranteed to transform your life" make experienced buyers suspicious. Specific, honest statements, such as "a printable weekly layout you can fill in during a fifteen-minute Sunday review," persuade better because they are easy to believe.

Writers also tend to bury the call to action. Each section of a page should lead naturally to the next step, and the final section should state clearly what to do and what the buyer receives. A buyer who reads the whole page and cannot find the button is a lost sale.

A worked skeleton for a planner sales page

Suppose you sell a printable weekly planner for freelancers. Using PAS, the page might open by naming the problem: deadlines scattered across email, invoices forgotten, and weeks that begin without a plan. The agitation section describes what happens when this continues, such as missed payments and evenings spent catching up. The solution section introduces the planner, explains its layout in plain terms, and shows a preview. Only after that should the page list the format, page count and price, followed by a short closing paragraph that repeats the outcome.

Using BAB for the same product would start with the reader's current week, describe the calmer week that becomes possible, and then bridge the two with the planner's structure. The facts are identical, but the emotional route is different. Testing both versions on the same audience is the most reliable way to learn which route your buyers prefer.

How to test copy without guessing

Change one major element at a time. If you test two frameworks at once, you will not know which change caused a difference. Track the conversion from visit to purchase, and watch the metrics the creator cheatsheet highlights, such as click-through rate and the share of visitors who sign up. Run each version long enough to collect a meaningful number of visits before deciding. A small, honest test beats a long debate about which paragraph sounds better.
