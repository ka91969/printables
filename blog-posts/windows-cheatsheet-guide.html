Windows Cheatsheet Guide: Three Pages of Hotkeys Explained

« Back to all articles

Windows offers a large number of keyboard shortcuts, and most people learn only a handful by accident. The Printable Windows Keyboard Shortcuts Cheatsheet collects the most useful of them into three pages, grouped by what you are trying to do. It is designed for power users, system administrators, software developers, IT professionals and everyday PC users, and it is a single digital PDF sized for US Letter. The cheatsheet is listed at €5.00 on its product page. This guide explains each volume, how to learn the shortcuts without being overwhelmed, and how to use the sheet at a desk.

Volume one: system infrastructure and virtual layouts

The first page covers system control and shell navigation. It includes Win plus I for Settings, Win plus E for File Explorer, Win plus X for the Power User menu, Win plus R for the Run dialogue, Ctrl plus Shift plus Esc for Task Manager and Win plus L to lock the screen. The same page covers window snapping and multitasking: Win plus Left or Right to snap a window, Win plus Z for snap layouts, Win plus D to show the desktop and Alt plus Tab to switch windows. It ends with virtual workspaces, where Win plus Ctrl plus D creates a desktop, Win plus Ctrl plus Left or Right moves between them, and Win plus Ctrl plus F4 closes the current one. These are the shortcuts that change how you organise the screen, so they deliver the biggest gains for most people.

Volume two: text editing, utilities and accessibility

The second page focuses on text and tools. It covers cursor movement with Ctrl plus arrow keys and selection with Ctrl plus Shift plus Home or End. It covers the clipboard history shortcut, Win plus V, which lets you paste something copied earlier. Screen capture appears here too, with Win plus Shift plus S for a region capture, PrtScn and Win plus G for the Game Bar and screen recorder. The page also covers accessibility shortcuts, including Win plus plus for the Magnifier, Win plus Ctrl plus Enter for Narrator and Sticky Keys. Knowing the accessibility shortcuts is useful for everyone, because they help when you are presenting on a large screen or supporting someone who relies on them.

Volume three: terminal, browsers and Run commands

The third page covers the terminal, browsers and system management commands. For Windows Terminal and PowerShell, it lists tab shortcuts such as Ctrl plus Shift plus T and W, split panes with Alt plus Shift plus plus and minus, and pane navigation. Browser shortcuts include Ctrl plus T and W, Ctrl plus Shift plus T, tab jumps with Ctrl plus 1 through 9, and Ctrl plus Shift plus N or P for private browsing. The page closes with Win plus R commands, including cmd, PowerShell, wt, sysdm.cpl, devmgmt.msc, ncpa.cpl, msconfig, resmon and services.msc. The context of each shortcut matters: Ctrl plus Shift plus T means reopen a closed tab in a browser, but opens a new tab in the terminal.

A learning plan that avoids overload

The full sheet contains many shortcuts, and trying to learn them all at once rarely works. A better approach is to choose three shortcuts from the first page that match your daily work, use them deliberately for a week, and then add three more. Practise them during real tasks rather than in isolation. A common path is to start with Win plus Left, Win plus V and Win plus Shift plus S, since these save time on almost every day. Once those feel automatic, move to virtual desktops and then to the terminal and Run commands.

Using the Run commands responsibly

The Win plus R commands on the third page are powerful, and they deserve care. Services.msc and msconfig can change how Windows starts and runs, and a careless change can make a computer harder to use. Open a management window, read the options, and change only what you understand. Note the current setting before changing it, so you can restore it if something goes wrong. For work on shared or managed machines, follow your organisation's policies before changing system settings.

Printing and using the sheet at your desk

Print the cheatsheet on standard US Letter paper. The layout is clean and ink-friendly, so black and white printing is usually enough. Pin the sheet above the monitor, place it under the keyboard, or keep it in a tech reference binder. If you prefer a tablet, the PDF opens in GoodNotes, Notability and Xodo, where you can mark the shortcuts you want to focus on. A printed sheet is most useful when it is visible at the moment of need, so choose the position that you will actually look at.

Keeping the sheet accurate

Windows updates occasionally change how a feature behaves, and shortcuts can differ between versions. Test the key shortcuts on your own machine before relying on them, and review the sheet every few months. Remove the shortcuts you never use, and add the ones that have become part of your routine. A shorter, accurate reference is more useful than a long one that no longer matches your system.

Is the Windows cheatsheet right for you?

The cheatsheet suits anyone who spends many hours at a Windows PC and wants to work faster with the keyboard. It is especially valuable for administrators and developers, who use the Run commands and terminal shortcuts often. It is less necessary for occasional users, although the first page alone is a quick way to save time. At the time of writing it is listed at €5.00.

Building a personal list from the three pages

Once you have worked through the sheet, make your own short list of the twelve shortcuts you use most, and keep it separate from the printed sheet. The personal list reflects your actual work, which is different for a developer, an administrator and a writer. Review it once a quarter and swap in any shortcut you have started to use without thinking. The printed sheet is the complete reference, and the personal list is the working set you practise every day.
