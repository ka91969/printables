How FICO Scores Work: The Five Factors and What Moves Them

« Back to all articles

A credit score is a summary of how you have handled borrowed money, reduced to a three-digit number. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve a loan and what rate to offer. Understanding what the score measures makes it much easier to influence. The Personal Finance Minimalist Cheatsheet breaks down the FICO Score 8 algorithm on its third page. This guide walks through the same factors with practical context.

This article is educational and not financial advice. Your credit file may differ from the examples here.

The five factors and their approximate weights

FactorApproximate weightWhat it measures
Payment history35%Whether you pay on time, including late payments, defaults and collections
Amounts owed30%How much you owe, especially credit utilization
Length of credit history15%The age of your accounts and your average account age
New credit10%Recent applications and newly opened accounts
Credit mix10%The variety of account types, such as cards, installment loans and mortgages

These weights are the approximate figures published for the general FICO model. Lenders can use other scores or their own models.

Payment history: the biggest lever

A single payment more than 30 days late can lower a score noticeably and stay on your report for years. Automating at least the minimum payment on every account is the simplest protection. If you have missed payments, the impact fades over time as long as new payments are on time.

Utilization: the fastest factor to change

Credit utilization is your revolving balance divided by your total credit limit. Lower is generally better. Many guides suggest keeping it below 30 percent, and people with the highest scores often stay far lower. Utilization is calculated from the balances reported on your statements, not necessarily the balance on your due date, so paying down a card before the statement closes can lower the reported figure. The cheatsheet calls this the statement date trick.

Credit age and mix

Older accounts help, which is one reason to keep an old card open with no annual fee rather than closing it. A healthy mix of revolving and installment credit also helps, but you should never open a loan you do not need just to improve the mix.

New credit

Each hard inquiry from a credit application can lower a score slightly for a short time. Multiple inquiries for the same loan type within a short window, such as rate shopping for a car or mortgage, are typically counted as one inquiry in FICO models.

Score tiers

FICO categorizes scores into ranges:

  • Poor: 300 to 579
  • Fair: 580 to 669
  • Good: 670 to 739
  • Very good: 740 to 799
  • Exceptional: 800 to 850

Moving from one tier to the next often matters more for loan pricing than small changes within a tier.

Check your own report

Errors on credit reports are common. In the United States you can request free reports from each bureau through AnnualCreditReport.com. Review them for accounts you do not recognize, incorrect balances and late payments that were actually on time. Dispute errors directly with the bureau that reports them.

Separating credit myths from practical facts

Credit scores attract a great deal of folk wisdom, and much of it is inaccurate. Some people believe that checking their own score lowers it, which is not the case when they check through a consumer service. Others assume that carrying a balance on a card improves the score, when paying in full is usually better and costs nothing in interest. Understanding the real mechanics of the score lets you focus on the actions that matter and ignore the rest.

The most important point is that a score reflects patterns over time. A single decision rarely determines it. Consistent, on-time payments and sensible use of available credit are what move the number, and those habits are within your control.

The mix of credit and why it matters

Credit mix refers to the variety of account types on your report, such as revolving credit cards and installment loans for cars or education. It carries a smaller weight than payment history or utilisation, so it should not drive borrowing decisions. Taking out a loan you do not need to diversify your report is rarely sensible. The better principle is to manage the accounts you already have, and to add new types only when they serve a genuine purpose.

Building a twelve-month plan

A practical plan spreads the work across a year. In the first month, pull your reports from each bureau and check them for errors. Set up automatic payments on every account, so that no payment is missed by accident. In the following months, reduce revolving balances where you can, starting with the card closest to its limit. Keep older accounts open where they carry no annual fee, because account age supports the score. Review the reports again after six months and again at the end of the year. Progress is usually visible within that time, though the exact pace depends on your starting point.

Why score tiers matter for borrowing

Lenders use score ranges to set pricing, so moving from one tier to the next can change the cost of a mortgage or a car loan. The tiers in this guide are a useful way to set goals. Rather than chasing a single number, aim to reach the next band, because that is where the practical benefit usually appears. Keep in mind that lenders apply their own criteria, and a higher score does not guarantee approval or a particular rate.

Understanding how new accounts affect the score

Opening a new account can lower a score briefly, because the new account is young and the hard inquiry is recent. The effect usually fades over months, provided payments remain on time. The practical lesson is to plan applications rather than submitting several at once. If a large purchase is coming, consider the timing of any new credit against the date of the application, so the score has time to recover before a lender reviews it.
