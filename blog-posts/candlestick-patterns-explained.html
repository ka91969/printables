Candlestick Patterns Explained: What Each Shape Shows and What It Cannot Tell You

« Back to all articles

Every candlestick on a chart summarizes one period of trading: where price opened, where it closed, and how high and low it went. Traders have named the shapes that recur, and the names are useful shorthand. They are also easy to misread. A pattern describes what happened in a bar, and it can hint at a shift in momentum. It cannot promise what happens next. This guide explains the most common patterns, what each one means, and where they fall short. Reading a single candle

A candle has a body, the range between open and close, and wicks, the thin lines showing the high and low. A green or white body usually means price closed higher than it opened. A red or dark body means it closed lower. Long wicks show that price travelled far from the body before returning, which often signals rejection.

Hammer and shooting star

Both have a small body and a long wick, and they differ in where the body sits and which direction the wick points.

  • Hammer: a small body near the top with a long lower wick, typically after a decline. The long lower wick shows sellers pushed price down, but buyers recovered it. Traders often wait for the next candle to confirm the move.
  • Shooting star: a small body near the bottom with a long upper wick, typically after an advance. Buyers pushed price up, but sellers drove it back down.

The shape is the same whether the market is stocks or crypto. What matters is the context: the trend before the pattern, and the volume and levels around it.

Bullish and bearish engulfing

An engulfing pattern uses two candles. A bullish engulfing occurs when a green body completely covers the previous red body, after a downtrend. A bearish engulfing is the reverse: a red body covering a prior green body after an uptrend. The second candle shows that one side took control decisively, which is why traders watch it. Like every pattern here, it needs confirmation and an exit plan.

Morning star

A morning star is a three-candle pattern. It starts with a large red candle, followed by a small-bodied candle that shows indecision, and then a large green candle that closes well into the first body. It is typically read as a possible bottom after a decline. The sequence is more informative than any single candle, but it still does not guarantee a reversal.

Doji

A doji forms when the open and close are almost equal, leaving a cross or plus-sign shape. It shows indecision. A doji after a long trend can suggest the trend is losing momentum, while a doji in a quiet market means very little. Context decides its meaning.

Why no pattern is a prediction

Patterns are descriptions, not forecasts. The same shape appears in rising markets, falling markets and sideways markets, and many of those occurrences lead nowhere. Traders who rely on a single pattern lose money in the cases that fail. Better practice is to combine patterns with a view of the trend, key price levels, volume and a defined risk per trade.

A disciplined approach

  1. Identify the trend first. A hammer means more after a clear decline than in sideways chop.
  2. Wait for confirmation, such as the next candle closing in the expected direction.
  3. Define where you would be wrong before you enter, and size the position so a loss is affordable.
  4. Record each pattern you trade in a journal, including the outcome, so you can measure it honestly.

Test before you trust

Before using any pattern with money, review how it performed on historical charts and then in a demo account. Many patterns look convincing in hindsight and disappoint in real time. Testing shows you the hit rate and the typical loss, which is more useful than the name of the pattern.

Why context changes the meaning of a pattern

A hammer appearing at the bottom of a long decline means something different from the same shape appearing in a flat, directionless market. Context includes the trend that preceded the pattern, the key price levels nearby, and the volume during the candle. Traders who read a pattern in isolation often misjudge it. The discipline is to ask what the market was doing before the candle formed and what the next few candles confirm, rather than reacting to the shape alone.

Volume as a second opinion

Volume records how much trading took place during each candle, and it can support or weaken a pattern. A bullish engulfing bar on heavy volume suggests that more participants took part in the move, which many traders treat as more meaningful than the same bar on light volume. Volume does not confirm a pattern on its own, but it adds weight to the reading. When volume and price disagree, the disagreement is worth investigating before you act.

Timeframes and the risk of noise

The same pattern looks different on a one-minute chart and a daily chart. Short timeframes produce many more patterns, and most of them are noise. Longer timeframes produce fewer patterns, but each one carries more weight. Choose a timeframe that matches your strategy, and be consistent. Switching timeframes to find a pattern you like is a common way to convince yourself of a trade that the data does not support.

Building a pattern library you can trust

A practical way to learn patterns is to build a library of examples from your own chart history. Mark each occurrence, note the trend and the outcome over a fixed period, and calculate how often the pattern led to the expected move. Your library will show which patterns work in your markets and which do not. This evidence-based approach is far more reliable than relying on a textbook description, and it gives you the honest feedback that a pattern name alone cannot provide.

Keeping emotions out of a pattern trade

A pattern can tempt traders to act before the evidence arrives, especially when a familiar shape appears and they feel they recognise a moment. Waiting for confirmation, writing down the trade plan in advance and accepting a small loss when the plan fails all help keep emotion in check. A pattern is a tool for organising decisions, and it works best when the decision is made calmly, in advance, rather than in the heat of the moment.
