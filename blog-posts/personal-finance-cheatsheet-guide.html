Personal Finance Cheatsheet Guide: FIRE, Budgets and Credit

« Back to all articles

Personal finance involves three questions that many people never answer clearly: how much is enough to stop working, how should money be divided each month, and how does credit affect the cost of borrowing. The Personal Finance Minimalist Cheatsheet addresses all three across three printable pages. It is aimed at wealth builders, people pursuing financial independence, young professionals and anyone who wants a clean reference for money decisions. It is a single digital PDF sized for US Letter, and it is listed at €5.00 on its product page. This guide explains each page, how to use the sheet monthly, and what to keep in mind.

This guide is educational and does not constitute financial advice. Consider speaking with a qualified adviser about your own situation.

Page one: the FIRE formula and wealth acceleration

The first page covers the core financial independence math. It explains the Rule of 25 and the 4 percent safe withdrawal rate, which draws on the Trinity Study, and it compares savings rates of 10, 25, 50 and 70 percent with the years needed to reach a target. It also describes four archetypes, Lean, Fat, Barista and Coast, which represent different ways of combining saving and work. The page includes the compound interest formula and a three-step action plan: tracking net worth, using broad index funds and making use of tax-advantaged accounts such as 401k, IRA and HSA plans. The sheet names specific index funds as examples, and that is not a recommendation for any particular investment. Choose investments based on your own circumstances and advice.

Page two: budgeting systems and allocation

The second page covers four budgeting systems: the 50/30/20 rule, zero-based budgeting, pay yourself first, and the envelope system. It then matches these systems to four money personalities: minimalists, strategists, over-spenders and beginners. The matching is the most useful part of the page, because the best system is the one you will actually follow. A minimalist may prefer pay yourself first, while someone who overspends in particular categories often benefits from envelopes. Choose one system for a trial period of three months, then review it honestly.

Page three: credit score factors and tiers

The third page breaks down the FICO Score 8 algorithm into its factors: payment history, credit utilisation, credit age, credit mix and new credit. It lists five credit tactics, including the statement date trick, authorised user arrangements, goodwill letters, requests for credit limit increases and bureau dispute workflows. Some of these tactics depend on the cooperation of lenders or bureaus, and some carry risks. Approach them carefully, understand what each one actually changes, and consider speaking with a reputable credit counsellor before acting. The page also gives tier benchmarks from Fair to Top Tier, which help set goals.

A monthly routine with the cheatsheet

A practical routine uses the sheet once a month. On the first day, calculate your savings rate and check it against the timelines on page one. Confirm your budgeting system is still working, and adjust any category that has overrun. Review your credit utilisation and payment history, and make sure every payment is scheduled. Once a quarter, check net worth and compare it with your targets. The routine takes about an hour a month, and it keeps the sheet useful rather than decorative.

Printing and using the sheet

Print on standard US Letter paper. The design is clean and ink-friendly, so a black and white print is usually enough. Many people keep the sheet in a money binder or pin it beside a desk where they pay bills. If you prefer a tablet, the PDF opens in GoodNotes, Notability and Xodo, where you can annotate the sections with your own numbers. A printed copy is especially useful during budget reviews, because it keeps the frameworks in front of you while you work.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is treating the FIRE target as a guarantee. The 4 percent rule is a planning guide based on historical data, and future conditions may differ. A second mistake is choosing a budgeting system by its popularity rather than its fit. A third is pursuing credit tactics without understanding them. Read each one carefully, and make sure the change you want is realistic for your situation.

Is the Personal Finance cheatsheet right for you?

The cheatsheet suits people who want a concise reference for budgeting, credit and long-term planning, and anyone starting to think seriously about financial independence. It is not a substitute for personalised advice, particularly for tax, retirement or investment decisions. Use it to frame the questions you bring to a professional, and to keep your monthly routine on track. At the time of writing it is listed at €5.00.

Why the three pages should be read together

It is tempting to treat the three pages as unrelated: one about retirement, one about budgets and one about credit. In practice they depend on each other. The savings rate on page one is only achievable if the budget on page two frees the money to save. Credit on page three affects the cost of the debts that a budget must service, and a high borrowing cost slows any plan for financial independence. Reading the three pages together reveals how a single change, such as reducing revolving balances, can improve cash flow, borrowing costs and the savings rate at the same time.

Adjusting the plan after a life change

Life changes, and the plan should change with it. A new job, a move, a family addition or a period of reduced income each affects the budget, the savings rate and the timeline on page one. When a change happens, rerun the savings rate calculation, check whether the budgeting system still suits the new situation, and review the credit position. Treat the cheatsheet as a set of tools for re-planning, not as a one-time calculation made at the start.
