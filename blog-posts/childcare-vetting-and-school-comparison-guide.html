Choosing Childcare or a New School: A Parent’s Vetting Checklist

« Back to all articles

Choosing who cares for your child, and where your child learns, are two of the biggest decisions a family makes. Both involve many details that are easy to forget under pressure: references, schedules, allergies, pickup rules, and the feeling you get after a visit. A written checklist turns that pressure into a repeatable process. The Family Planner provides five printable templates for exactly this work.

Vetting a babysitter or nanny

Interview every candidate with the same list, so you can compare them fairly. A thorough checklist covers:

  1. Background checks and references. Ask whether the candidate has been checked, and call past families, not just the names provided.
  2. CPR and first aid certification. Ask for the certificate and its expiry date.
  3. Experience with your child's age group. A caregiver who manages toddlers well may be unprepared for school-age children, and the reverse is also true.
  4. Schedule and rates. Confirm availability, cancellation policy and what happens when your plans change.
  5. Discipline and values. Describe your approach to screens, bedtimes and conflict, and ask how they would handle the same situations.
  6. Emergency procedures and allergies. Ask what they would do in a medical emergency and whether they know how to use your home's emergency information.
  7. Trial visit. Arrange a supervised visit before the first full day, and note how your child responds.

Finish with a short notes section for your gut feeling. Write it down the same day, while the impression is still clear.

Comparing up to three schools or daycares

Side-by-side comparison beats memory, especially when the options are all good. Use one column per option and score each factor:

  • Tuition and total cost, including extras such as meals and activities
  • Location and commute, measured at the times you will actually travel
  • Class size and staff turnover
  • Curriculum or philosophy, and how it fits your child's learning style
  • Safety, security and facilities
  • Reviews and reputation, read with the awareness that a few reviews can be unrepresentative
  • Schedule fit, including early drop-off and late pickup

Add a 1 to 5 gut-feeling score for each option, and write one sentence on what would make you choose it. The sentence often clarifies the decision more than the scores do.

Preparing an emergency information sheet

Anyone who cares for your child, from a babysitter to a grandparent, should have a single sheet with everything they need in an emergency: your child's date of birth, address, pediatrician contact, emergency contacts, known allergies, current medications, insurance details, and the list of people authorized to pick up. Include notes on comfort items and routines, such as the bedtime story or the nap-time blanket. Keep one copy in your bag and one with your child's caregivers, and review it whenever something changes.

Keeping the records current

Schedule a reminder to review the emergency sheet every three months, and update it after any change in medication, allergies, address or contacts. Records that are out of date are a common source of avoidable problems.

Preparing questions before the first conversation

Parents often walk into a childcare interview with a general sense of what they want and leave without the answers they needed. Writing questions in advance changes the outcome. Decide what matters most to your family, whether that is experience with a particular age group, flexibility with schedules, or a shared approach to discipline, and draft questions that test those priorities. The checklist in the Family Planner gives you a starting structure, but the most useful questions are the ones that reflect your child's routine and your household's values.

Ask open questions that invite stories rather than yes or no answers. Asking how a candidate would respond to a child who refuses to eat lunch reveals far more than asking whether they are patient. Listen for specifics, for calm reasoning and for a willingness to say what they do not know.

Recognising the warning signs

Most caregivers are caring people, but some interviews raise concerns. Be alert to candidates who dismiss your questions about safety, who cannot give a clear answer about emergency procedures, or who become defensive when you ask for references. Pay attention to how the candidate speaks about children and families they have worked with. Respect and warmth are important, and so is discretion. A candidate who gossips about previous employers is likely to gossip about your family as well.

Trust your observations, and record them. The notes section of the checklist exists for this reason. Your immediate impression is valuable, but it is more useful when it is written down alongside the facts.

Setting expectations in writing

Once you choose a caregiver, agree on expectations in writing. Cover hours, pay, notice periods for changes, rules about visitors and screens, dietary requirements, medication handling and what to do in an emergency. A written agreement protects both sides, and it prevents the small misunderstandings that grow into conflicts. Review it after the first month, because early experience often reveals details that were not obvious during the interview.

Comparing schools and daycares with a clear head

The school and daycare comparison sheet works best when you fill it in after visiting each option rather than from brochures alone. Visit at a normal time of day, not during a special event, so you see the everyday routine. Watch how staff speak to children, how transitions between activities are handled and whether the children appear engaged. Your gut feeling, recorded on the sheet, is a legitimate factor, as long as it sits alongside concrete information such as cost, location and staffing.

Preparing the emergency information sheet well

An emergency sheet is only useful if it is accurate and easy to find. Keep a printed copy in a consistent place, store a digital version somewhere your caregivers can reach quickly, and review it each season. Include the names of people authorised to collect your child, and make sure the list matches what the school or daycare holds on file. Mismatched records cause delays at exactly the moments when speed matters most.
