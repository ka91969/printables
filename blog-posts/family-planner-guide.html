Family Planner Guide: Childcare, Schools and Emergency Records

« Back to all articles

Decisions about childcare and schooling carry a lot of weight, and they are easy to make badly when the details are scattered across notes, messages and memory. The Family Planner gathers the practical side of those decisions into one printable set. It contains five minimalist pages in a single digital PDF, sized for US Letter, and it is designed for parents, guardians and caregivers who want to keep family logistics organised. The planner is currently listed at €5.00 on its product page. This guide walks through each page, shows how to use the planner when you are choosing a babysitter or comparing schools, and explains how to keep the records current.

Page one: the childcare vetting checklist

The first page is a childcare vetting checklist that you can use in every interview. It covers background checks, CPR and first aid certification, experience with your child's age group, schedule availability, rates, discipline alignment, emergency procedures, allergies, trial visits, backup arrangements and a space for gut-feeling notes. The value of a checklist is that every candidate is measured against the same questions. Interviews often feel warm and persuasive in the moment, and a written list keeps the comparison fair. Complete the notes section as soon as the interview ends, while your impressions are still clear.

Page two: the school and daycare comparison

The second page lets you compare up to three schools or daycares side by side. The factors include tuition and cost, location and commute, class size, curriculum or philosophy, staff turnover, safety and security, facilities, reviews and reputation, and schedule fit. It also includes a gut-feeling score from one to five and space for final decision notes. Completing the comparison after visiting each option, rather than from brochures alone, gives you much better information. Visit at a normal time of day so that you see the everyday routine, not a special event.

Page three: the child emergency information sheet

The third page is the child emergency information sheet, which is the document every babysitter, grandparent and daycare provider should have. It holds the child's date of birth, address, pediatrician contact, emergency contacts, known allergies, medications, insurance information, the list of people authorised to pick up, and notes on comfort items and routines. Keep a printed copy in a consistent place, store a digital version somewhere your caregivers can reach quickly, and review it each season. Compare the authorised pickup list with what your school or daycare holds on file, because mismatches cause delays at the moments when speed matters most.

Page four: the milestone tracker

The fourth page is a milestone tracker with space for the child's name, the milestone, the age or date it was reached, and a note about the memory. It is a simple record that becomes valuable over years. Use it for first words, first steps, new skills and firsts that matter to your family. The tracker is not a developmental checklist, and it does not replace advice from a pediatrician. It is a record of your child's own story, kept in your own words.

Page five: family rules and responsibilities

The final page sets out family rules, consequences and rewards, and an age-appropriate responsibilities table with columns for the child, the responsibility and how often it applies. Clear rules are easier to keep when they are written down and agreed in advance. Involve older children in drafting the rules, because a rule they helped to shape is more likely to be followed. Review the chart every few months, since responsibilities that suit a seven-year-old will change as the child grows.

A routine for choosing childcare or a school

Start with the vetting checklist for any babysitter or nanny, and complete it for every candidate you interview. Use the comparison sheet when you are choosing a daycare or school, and make at least one unannounced visit to each option if the setting allows it. Once you have chosen a caregiver or school, complete the emergency information sheet and share it with everyone who will look after your child. Revisit the family rules page once the new arrangement has settled, and adjust the responsibilities to fit the new routine.

Printing and using the planner in practice

Print on standard US Letter paper. The pages are black and white and designed to save ink, so a home printer is usually enough. Many families keep the emergency sheet in a clear sleeve in the front of a binder, and print a fresh copy each time a detail changes. If you prefer tablets, the PDF opens in apps such as GoodNotes, Notability and Xodo, where you can complete the forms digitally. Keep the originals in a safe place, especially the emergency information and any records that include medical details.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent mistake is treating the emergency sheet as finished once it is written. Records go out of date quickly, particularly medications, allergies and contact numbers, so schedule a review every three months. A second mistake is letting the vetting checklist become a formality. The notes section matters, and a candidate who answers every question smoothly but cannot give a clear example from past work deserves a second look. A third mistake is making decisions from a single visit when a second visit would clarify the picture.

Is the Family Planner right for you?

The Family Planner suits parents who make childcare and school decisions, families who share care with grandparents or friends, and anyone who needs a clear emergency record for a child. It is less useful for daily routines, which the Parent Planner covers in more detail. Many families use both: the Family Planner for decisions and handovers, and the Parent Planner for the day-to-day rhythm of family life.
