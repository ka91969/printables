Finance, Trading and Business Cheatsheet Guide: Five Modules

« Back to all articles

Finance has a reputation for complexity, but most of its core ideas can be organised onto a small set of pages. The Finance, Trading and Business Cheatsheet does exactly that. In two printable pages organised as five modules, it covers personal finance, active trading, corporate valuation, cost of capital and master formulas, along with playbooks by role. It is designed for investors, day traders, business founders, financial analysts and corporate finance students, and it is a single digital PDF sized for US Letter. The cheatsheet is listed at €5.00 on its product page. This guide explains each module and how to use the sheet in study or work.

This guide is educational and does not constitute investment or financial advice.

Module one: personal wealth and financial planning

The first module covers the 50/30/20 framework, which the sheet describes as needs, wants and wealth. It also includes exponential growth, the Rule of 72 as a quick way to estimate doubling time, compound interest, dollar-cost averaging, the debt avalanche and debt snowball methods, and asset allocation across conservative, moderate and aggressive profiles. The module connects everyday money decisions with the formulas that explain them, so a student or a saver can see why a small change in return or time matters so much.

Module two: active trading and risk management

The second module covers risk and technical analysis. It includes the 1 percent risk rule, the risk-to-reward ratio, the position sizing formula, common chart patterns, the 50 and 200-day moving averages, and momentum indicators such as RSI and MACD. The most important part of this module is the risk framework, not the pattern names. Trading carries a substantial risk of loss, and the risk rules are what keep a trader in the game long enough to learn. Test any approach carefully before using real money.

Module three: corporate finance and valuation models

The third module covers the main valuation approaches: discounted cash flow, comparable company analysis and precedent transactions. It includes key multiples such as P/E, P/S, EV/EBITDA and P/B. Each method answers a different question. A discounted cash flow estimates intrinsic value from future cash, comparable analysis compares a business with similar firms, and precedent transactions look at prices paid in past deals. Experienced analysts use several methods together and explain the gaps between them, rather than trusting a single number.

Module four: cost of capital and master formulas

The fourth module collects formulas that appear across finance: the weighted average cost of capital, the capital asset pricing model, enterprise value, the Sharpe ratio, compound annual growth, break-even analysis, net present value and free cash flow. The formulas are most useful when read as questions. What discount rate reflects the risk? What is the business worth before its debt is considered? How much return did the investment earn for each unit of risk? The sheet gives the formulas, and the questions give them meaning.

Module five: playbooks by role

The final module offers playbooks for beginner savers, active traders, business founders and value investors. Each playbook suggests the priorities and habits that suit that role. A beginner saver may focus on budgeting and steady contributions, while a founder may focus on cash flow and unit economics. The playbooks help readers choose which parts of the sheet matter most for their situation.

Using the sheet in study and at work

Students can use the sheet to check formulas during problem sets, and to connect each formula with the question it answers. Professionals can keep it near their workstation when preparing a valuation, reviewing a budget or discussing a deal. A useful habit is to work one example from each module by hand, then check the result in a spreadsheet. The exercise builds judgement that a formula sheet alone cannot provide.

Printing and tablet use

Print the sheet on standard US Letter paper. The layout is minimalist and prints clearly in black and white, so it works well on a desk or in a study binder. If you prefer a tablet, the PDF opens in GoodNotes, Notability and Xodo, where you can annotate the formulas with your own assumptions. Keep a copy of the sheet next to any spreadsheet you use for modelling.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is applying a formula without checking its inputs. A discount rate expressed as an annual figure must match annual cash flows, and enterprise value and equity value answer different questions. A second mistake is trusting a valuation without testing its sensitivity to the key assumptions. A third is treating the risk rules in the trading module as optional. They are the part of the sheet that protects capital.

Is the Finance, Trading and Business cheatsheet right for you?

The sheet suits finance students, analysts and business owners who want a compact reference across several related areas, and it is valuable for anyone who wants to understand the logic behind common financial formulas. It is not a course, and it does not replace professional advice on investments, taxes or business decisions. At the time of writing it is listed at €5.00.

Connecting the modules to a real decision

The modules become more useful when they are applied to a decision. Imagine you are considering whether to buy a small business. The personal wealth module helps you judge how much of your savings the purchase should use. The valuation module gives you methods for estimating what the business is worth. The cost of capital module helps you set the discount rate, and the role playbooks remind you of the priorities that matter most for a founder or a buyer. Working through one real decision uses every module and shows how the formulas connect in practice.
