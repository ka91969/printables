Financial Statements Explained: Income, Balance Sheet and Cash Flow

« Back to all articles

Every company, large or small, reports its finances through three statements. Investors, lenders and managers read them to answer the same question: is this business healthy? Each statement answers a different part of that question, and they only make sense together. This guide explains what each one shows, how they connect, and why reading them carefully matters. The valuation formulas that use these numbers are on the Finance, Trading & Business Cheatsheet.

This article is educational and not investment advice.

The income statement: performance over a period

The income statement shows what a business earned and spent over a period, usually a quarter or a year. It starts with revenue, subtracts the cost of the goods or services sold to reach gross profit, then subtracts operating costs such as salaries, rent and marketing. Interest and taxes come next, and the result is net income, often called the bottom line.

The income statement answers: did the business make money over this period? It is a record of activity, not a snapshot.

The balance sheet: position at a single moment

The balance sheet is a snapshot at one date. It lists what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and what is left for the owners (equity). The structure rests on one equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Every asset is financed either by borrowing, which creates a liability, or by the owners, through investment and retained earnings. The equation always balances, which is why it is a useful check when reading a statement.

The cash flow statement: where the cash went

The cash flow statement tracks the movement of cash in three categories:

  • Operating activities: cash from day-to-day business, such as customer receipts and supplier payments.
  • Investing activities: cash spent on or received from long-term assets, such as equipment or property.
  • Financing activities: cash from borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares or paying dividends.

The total of the three is the change in cash over the period. This statement is where you see whether a business is generating cash or consuming it.

Why profit is not the same as cash

A business can report a profit and still run short of money. Suppose a firm sells goods on credit in December. The income statement records the sale immediately, but the customer may not pay for sixty days. Meanwhile, the firm must pay its suppliers and staff. The profit exists on paper, but the cash has not arrived. The cash flow statement reveals this gap, which is why experienced readers check it alongside the income statement.

How the three connect

The statements link in predictable ways. Net income from the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet. Changes in working capital, such as receivables and payables, show up as adjustments in the operating section of the cash flow statement. When you trace one item across all three, you see the business as a whole.

From statements to valuation

Analysts use these numbers as inputs to valuation. Free cash flow, which the cash flow statement helps estimate, feeds discounted cash flow models. Multiples such as EV/EBITDA draw on the income statement and balance sheet. The formulas are only as good as the statements behind them, so reading the statements carefully comes first.

A simple reading routine

  1. Start with the income statement to see revenue trends and profit margins.
  2. Check the balance sheet for debt levels and whether assets look reasonable.
  3. Read the cash flow statement to confirm that operating cash supports the reported profit.
  4. Look for mismatches between the three, and ask why they exist.

Ratios that turn statements into insight

Reading the statements line by line tells you what happened. Ratios help you judge how well it happened. Gross margin compares gross profit with revenue, showing how much the business keeps from each sale before operating costs. Net margin shows the share of revenue that becomes profit after all costs. Debt to equity compares borrowing with the owners' stake, indicating how much the business relies on lenders. The current ratio compares short-term assets with short-term liabilities, showing whether the business can meet obligations due within a year. Each ratio is only meaningful against a benchmark, such as the company's own history or its industry peers, so use them as comparisons rather than absolute tests.

What the footnotes reveal

The notes that accompany the financial statements often contain information that the main tables do not. They explain accounting choices, describe debt terms, list commitments and disclose significant risks. Readers who skip the notes miss much of the story. When a company changes how it recognises revenue or depreciates assets, the change may affect comparisons across years, and the notes are where that effect is explained. Make a habit of scanning the notes for changes, unusual items and anything described as uncertain.

Reading a statement in context

A single set of numbers means little without context. A company with heavy spending on equipment may show weak operating cash flow in a given year, while still investing for future growth. A business in a seasonal industry may look unusual in one quarter and normal over a full year. Compare the statements with several years of history, and with competitors where possible. Context turns a list of figures into a judgement about the business.

Why this skill matters beyond finance

Understanding financial statements is useful well beyond investing. Small business owners use them to decide whether to hire, borrow or expand. Managers use them to understand how their department contributes to the whole. Freelancers can read their own profit and loss in the same way. The statements are a common language for business, and learning to read them gives you a clearer view of any organisation you work with.
