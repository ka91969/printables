The 1% Risk Rule and Position Sizing for Active Traders

« Back to all articles

Most trading losses are not caused by bad entries. They come from position sizes that are too large, stops that move, and losses that spiral into revenge trades. Risk management is the discipline that prevents that spiral, and it is the part of trading that can be written down and followed. The Day Trading & Crypto Cheatsheet covers price action, indicators, options Greeks and risk rules across four pages. This guide focuses on the risk side.

Trading involves substantial risk of loss. This article is educational and not financial advice.

The 1% rule

The 1 percent rule limits the amount you can lose on any single trade to 1 percent of your account. On a $10,000 account, that is $100. It sounds conservative, but it means a string of ten losing trades costs about 10 percent of your capital, which is survivable, while a few oversized losses can end an account.

Position sizing, step by step

The sizing formula connects your risk limit to your entry and stop-loss:

Shares = (Account × Risk %) ÷ (Entry price − Stop-loss price)

Worked example: your account is $10,000, your risk is 1 percent ($100), you plan to buy at $50 and place a stop-loss at $48. The risk per share is $2, so the position is $100 ÷ $2 = 50 shares. If the stop is hit, you lose about $100 plus any slippage.

Notice what the formula does: a wider stop means fewer shares, and a tighter stop means more. Traders who skip this step often choose a share count first and then hope the stop is far enough away.

Risk-to-reward ratio

Risk-to-reward (R:R) compares what you can lose with what you aim to gain. In the example above, a 1 to 2 ratio would target a $4 gain per share, or $200 on 50 shares. Traders often skip setups that do not offer a reasonable ratio, but a higher ratio does not guarantee profit. Win rate and R:R work together, and you should track both in a journal.

Daily limits and behavior

  • Set a maximum daily drawdown. When you reach it, stop trading for the day.
  • Do not move a stop-loss to avoid a loss. Moving stops is one of the most common ways small losses become large ones.
  • Use a waiting period after a loss. Revenge trading usually happens within minutes of a loss, so step away first.
  • Keep a journal. Record entry, stop, target, reason and outcome, then review weekly.

Indicators in context

Common indicators include RSI (usually 14 periods), MACD, Bollinger Bands, VWAP and ATR. ATR is especially useful for stop placement, because it measures recent volatility. An indicator is a description of price, not a prediction of it. Use indicators to frame decisions, and let the risk formula determine size.

Leverage and derivatives

Leverage multiplies both gains and losses. In crypto derivatives, funding rates, liquidation prices and margin mode (cross versus isolated) can change your exposure quickly. Calculate your liquidation price before opening a leveraged position, and keep the 1 percent rule in force regardless of leverage.

Understanding drawdown and why it hurts

A drawdown is the decline from a peak in your account value to a later low. It matters more than most traders expect, because recovering from a loss requires a larger gain. A fifty percent loss needs a one hundred percent gain to return to the starting point. This asymmetry is the strongest argument for the one percent rule and for daily loss limits. Small, controlled losses keep you in the game, while large losses force you to take bigger risks to recover, which often leads to further losses.

When you set risk rules, think in terms of the worst realistic run rather than the average outcome. Ask how many losing trades in a row you could survive, and size your positions so that a bad week is painful but not destructive.

Keeping a trading journal that teaches you something

A journal turns experience into learning. For each trade, record the setup, the reason for entry, the planned stop and target, the actual outcome and a short note on how you felt. Review the journal weekly. Patterns usually appear quickly: a particular setup may consistently underperform, or losses may cluster during a certain time of day. The journal's value lies in honesty, so write it immediately after each trade rather than reconstructing it later.

The psychology that undermines risk rules

Most traders know the rules and still break them. Moving a stop-loss to avoid a loss, adding to a losing position, or trading to recover a bad morning are common examples. These behaviours are driven by emotion, and no formula removes emotion entirely. The practical defence is to write the rules down before the session, keep them visible, and agree on a consequence for breaking them, such as stopping for the day. A rule that is written and reviewed is far harder to ignore than one that exists only in memory.

Leverage and risk that is easy to underestimate

Leverage multiplies both gains and losses, and it can turn a manageable position into an unmanageable one quickly. In derivative and crypto markets, funding rates, margin mode and liquidation prices affect risk in ways that are not obvious at first. Calculate the liquidation price before opening any leveraged position, and confirm that the position size still respects your one percent limit at that price. If the numbers do not work, reduce the size or stay out.

Creating a trading plan you can follow on a bad day

A trading plan is most valuable when markets are unfriendly, and that is precisely when it is hardest to follow. Write the plan in plain language, listing the markets you trade, the setups you use, the conditions that must be met before entry, and the rules for exits. Keep a copy beside your trading screen, and review it at the start of each session. A plan that is read before each trade is more likely to be followed than one that is only remembered.
