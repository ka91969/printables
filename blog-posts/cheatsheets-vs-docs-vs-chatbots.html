Documentation, Chatbots or a Printed Cheatsheet? Where Each One Belongs

« Back to all articles

There are now three ways to look something up at your desk: official documentation, an AI chatbot, and a printed cheatsheet. Most people use all three, usually without thinking about which one to reach for. That is where mistakes start. A chatbot answer is not a verified reference. A manual is not a quick lookup. A cheatsheet is not the place to learn a subject from scratch. My view is simple: each tool has a clear job, and using the right one saves time and prevents errors.

Official documentation: the authority, but slow

Official documentation is the source of truth. It is complete, maintained by the people who build the tool or publish the standard, and it covers edge cases that no summary includes. When you need to confirm exactly how a command behaves, or what a formula assumes, documentation is the right place to look.

Its weakness is speed. A manual is organized for completeness, not for the moment you need one command. Reading it while you are in the middle of a task interrupts your flow, and it is easy to get lost in related pages.

AI chatbots: fast explanations, unverified answers

Chatbots are excellent at explaining concepts in plain language, comparing options and drafting first versions of code or text. If you are new to a subject, a chatbot can give you a clear explanation in seconds, and follow-up questions cost you nothing.

The risk is confidence without verification. A chatbot can produce a plausible command that does not exist in your version, a formula with the wrong assumption, or a figure that is out of date. The answer reads well either way. For that reason I treat chatbot output as a draft, and check anything that matters against a trusted source. This is especially important for commands that change system settings and for any calculation that affects money.

Printed cheatsheets: the curated lookup

A printed cheatsheet is a reference you have already filtered. Someone decided which items belong on the page, and the layout puts them where your eye can find them quickly. It works without a screen, a connection or a browser tab, which removes the most common distractions.

Its limit is scope. A cheatsheet covers the lookups you repeat, not the full range of a subject, and it is not the right place to learn something you have never seen before.

Build a reference stack

A setup that works well combines the three tools in a clear order. Keep one printed cheatsheet for each recurring task. Bookmark the official documentation for the tools you depend on. Use a chatbot to explain new ideas and draft examples, then check the result against the documentation before it reaches your work. The chatbot speeds up learning, the cheatsheet speeds up repetition, and the documentation keeps you honest.

My verdict

Print what you use every week. Read the documentation for what matters. Use a chatbot to understand, and never to skip checking. If you apply that rule, you will spend less time searching and make fewer mistakes.

The cost of checking everything from scratch

Every time you stop to look up a command or a formula, you pay a small cost in attention. Individually the cost is trivial, but across a working day the interruptions add up, and each one breaks the thread of the task. A good reference reduces that cost by answering the question quickly, in a form you can read at a glance. The right choice of tool therefore affects not only accuracy but also the flow of your work.

Why a single source of truth is rarely enough

Many people try to rely on one source for everything. Some use only documentation and find it too slow for routine work. Others use only a chatbot and accept its answers without checking. Still others keep only a printed sheet and struggle when a question falls outside its scope. Each of these approaches has a blind spot. A layered approach, with a printed reference for routine work, documentation for accuracy and a chatbot for explanation, covers the gaps that each tool leaves on its own.

How to tell when an answer needs verifying

A simple rule is to verify any answer that could cause harm if it were wrong. Commands that change system settings, formulas that inform financial decisions and instructions that affect health deserve checking against an authoritative source. A question about the meaning of a concept or a suggestion for wording carries less risk, so a quick answer from a chatbot may be enough. Build this judgement into your routine, and you will know when speed is safe and when accuracy comes first.

Using documentation without drowning in it

Documentation is detailed, and that detail can be overwhelming. Read the introduction and the section that matches your task, then search within the page for the specific term you need. Keep a short note of the commands or settings you use most often, so you do not need to return to the full manual for every lookup. Over time, your personal notes become a reference shaped to your own work, which is often more useful than the original document.
